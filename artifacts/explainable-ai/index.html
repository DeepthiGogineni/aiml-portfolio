Introduction

This is my fourth portfolio artifact, for Workshop 4.4. In my Salesforce work I configure AI features such as Agentforce, and the question I hear most is 'why did the AI say that?'. This artifact looks at how researchers and companies try to answer that question for large language models (LLMs), and at how validation and performance metrics decide whether the answer can be trusted.

Description

One tall infographic in five sections: what explainable AI is and why it matters; 6 challenges sorted into three families; 8 techniques sorted by how much access they need, with a matrix that links each challenge to the techniques that answer it; 12 efforts from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind and Meta; and a validation section that includes a small experiment of my own.

Objective

To show a non-technical reader that an AI explanation is a claim that needs testing, to map the main ways of producing and checking explanations, and to show how the leading labs contribute.

Process

  1. Read the assignment resources, then the primary papers and lab posts behind each claim (29 sources, all listed in the graphic).
  2. Listed every challenge and technique I found, then sorted them: challenges by where the difficulty lives, techniques by the access they need.
  3. Built a challenge-by-technique matrix to find the relationships, and checked that every challenge has a main answer and every technique answers something.
  4. Ran a small experiment on the breast-cancer data from my report 4.1 to show how an explanation can be validated (deletion test).
  5. Put every item in one data file with 40 automatic checks, then generated the infographic, this page and the explanatory document from it.
  6. Reviewed the rendered image section by section for alignment, contrast and spelling, and fixed what I found.

Tools

  • Python and scikit-learn for the experiment (permutation importance and the deletion test).
  • HTML, CSS and Playwright to build the infographic as live text and export a PNG and a PDF.
  • Primary sources papers, lab research posts and regulation text, not blog summaries.
  • An AI assistant (Claude) to help draft the build scripts and first-pass wording. I checked each claim against its source and edited the text.

Value Proposition

It shows I can take a fast-moving, technical topic, check each claim against its source, and explain it to a mixed audience without overstating what is known. Teams adopting AI need people who can say what an explanation proves and what it does not.

Unique Value

Artifact 1 sorted algorithms, Artifact 2 explained a neural network and Artifact 3 priced LLM training. This one is about trust: it classifies challenges and solutions, maps how they relate, and includes my own test of an explanation. Every claim carries an evidence tag.

Relevance

Salesforce customers ask whether they can trust AI answers and who is accountable. Knowing that written reasoning can be unfaithful, that model cards document limits, and that metrics and explanation tests answer different questions helps me advise on human review, vendor questions and when an AI feature is safe to automate.

Validation and Metrics

How an Explanation Becomes Evidence

Four questions, in order. The last is where explainability meets validation, so I tested it on the breast-cancer data from my report 4.1.

Validation questions
#QuestionMetric or testHow
1Does it work?Accuracy, precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUCScore predictions against known answers.
2Does it work on new data?Held-out test set, cross-validationHold data back, or rotate it across folds.
3Does it hold up under pressure?Red teaming, bias tests, jailbreak ratesAttack it and test across groups.
4Is the explanation true?Deletion test, hint testRemove what the explanation names and see if the output really changes.

Both explanations pass: deleting the named features costs more recall than deleting random ones. Yet the two equally accurate models share only 3 of 5 top features (worst concave points, worst concavity, worst texture). Method: permutation importance on the 114-patient test set (42 malignant), deletion by replacing features with their training mean, scikit-learn, seed 42. Correlated features share importance, so treat the lists as indicative.

Classification

How I Sorted the Content

Challenges are sorted by where the difficulty lives; techniques by the access they need; relationships by a challenge-technique matrix.

Sorting Rules

  • Challenges: where the difficulty lives. Family A (the model is opaque) is about the model's insides. Family B (explanations can mislead) is about the explanation as an output. Family C (data and access) is about what surrounds the model.
  • Techniques: how much access they need. From the outside needs only inputs and outputs. From the inside needs the weights. Around the model needs documents and oversight. Access decides who can use a technique, so it is the most useful way to sort them.
  • Relationships: the matrix. A challenge-technique pair is marked 'main' when the technique was designed to address that difficulty, and 'partial' when it helps but cannot settle it.
  • Evidence. Claims tagged R come from the cited source. Tags D come from my own experiment. Numbers I could only find in secondary summaries are written qualitatively.

Decisions and Trade-offs

  • Sort challenges by cause, not by severity. Severity is subjective and changes. Cause stays stable and points to the fix: opaque insides need inside tools, misleading explanations need tests, and access problems need documentation and rules.
  • Sort techniques by access, not by method name. Method names (gradients, autoencoders) mean little to a non-technical reader. Access answers a practical question: what can I do with a closed model compared with an open one?
  • Chain-of-thought appears twice. It is a technique (T1) and also the subject of challenge C3, because the same written reasoning that helps can mislead. I placed it as a technique and mark it 'partial' for C3, since it is what the other methods check.
  • Attention appears twice. Attention views are a technique (T4) and a challenge (C4). The paper that criticises them is the reason they are marked partial for C4.
  • Validation gets its own section. The assignment treats validation and metrics as a separate pillar. I connected it to explainability with a fourth question, 'Is the explanation true?', and with a real test.

Corrections I Made While Building It

  • I first planned to quote exact faithfulness rates for reasoning models (such as 25% and 39%). They appeared only in a third-party summary, and the paper's abstract says rates are often below 20%, so I wrote it as 'less than 20% in most settings tested'.
  • Search results gave conflicting dates for the EU AI Act's high-risk rules, which have been revised. I kept only the date I could confirm (general-purpose AI duties from 2 August 2025).
  • I expected my two models' top features to agree closely. They share only 3 of 5, so I changed the experiment's message from 'the explanation is right' to 'it passes the test, and equally accurate models can still differ'.
  • A late render check showed the family letters in the challenge cards were invisible (text colour matched the background) and the 2x2 labels were misplaced. I fixed both.
Reflection

Artifact Reflection

Lessons Learned

What I learned. I started by assuming that asking a model to explain itself was a reasonable way to see its reasoning. The sources changed that. When a hint changed an answer, the models' reasoning often left it out, so a fluent explanation is not evidence. The second surprise was access: outsiders using a closed model are limited to prompts and perturbations, while the interesting inside-the-model work happens at the labs or on open models such as Gemma.

The hardest part. Sorting. Several items fit two families, such as chain-of-thought and attention. I had to decide whether a classification should be neat or truthful, and I chose to show the overlap and explain it. Evidence quality was the other challenge: some headline numbers existed only in summaries, and I chose to write them qualitatively rather than risk repeating an error.

What worked. Building the challenge-by-technique matrix. It exposed that no single technique answers all 6 challenges, which is the real message of the field. Running my own deletion test also helped: removing the five features each model named cost 11.9 and 9.5 points of recall, against 0.2 and 3.0 for random features. That gave me a concrete example of what 'validating an explanation' means.

Connections and next steps. The metrics section builds on my model comparison in report 4.1 (accuracy, precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC) and on the red-teaming and bias examples in report 4.3. At work I will use the four-question checklist when customers ask whether to trust an AI feature. Next I want to try a model-agnostic method such as SHAP on a text classifier and compare it with the deletion test.

Audience Customization

Written for classmates, instructors and business colleagues who use AI tools but do not build them. The key idea (an explanation is a claim to test) appears in the opening diagram, the experiment and the takeaways. Jargon is explained where it first appears, and colour is paired with letters and words.

Limitations

  • The lab efforts are examples, not a complete survey. The field changes quickly.
  • The experiment uses a small tabular dataset and a simple deletion method. It illustrates how to test an explanation; it does not test an LLM. Correlated features share importance, so the lists are indicative.
  • Many results come from single models (Claude 3.5 Haiku, Gemma 2, GPT-2) and short prompts, so they may not generalise.
  • The graphic cites papers and lab posts. I did not run the labs' methods myself, apart from the experiment.
